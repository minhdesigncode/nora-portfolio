01 the short version

I was the only designer, and the design helped win the deal

At a small health-tech startup I designed a platform that helps patients afford a long, expensive cancer treatment and stay on it. I worked straight from the client's compliance documents, with our PO and CTO, and my designs became the core of the founder's pitch.

02 context

A vendor that runs patient programs for pharma

Big pharma companies run patient programs: they help people who cannot afford a treatment get it at a lower price, and support them while they take it. They hire vendors like us to build the platform and run the operations.

The business goal is simple to say: more eligible patients start the treatment and stay on it. The hard part is how. Everything must feel like support, never like selling, or the program breaks the law.

03 the problem

Three users, ten documents, one wall

The brief was about 10 compliance documents, all text, handed to me by the founder and PO. From them I had to design one platform for three groups that must never mix:

The patient

Very sick, often on a phone, paying out of pocket. Needs to enrol fast and know the outcome.

The Access team

Checks eligibility and money, creates orders. Must never see clinical calls.

The Support team

Nurses who follow up on doses and check-ups. Must never see income or documents.

The real question every pharma asks first: can you prove that Access data and Support data stay apart? Few platforms can. That proof decides the deal.

04 my role

How I worked with the PO, CTO and founder

  • Me: the only designer, end to end: flows, UI, UX writing, branding.
  • PO: owned scope. We read the SOPs together and agreed what was in and out.
  • CTO: owned feasibility. Every flow I drew became a status he could build.
  • Founder: owned the client. My screens and flows became his pitch.
  • When we disagreed, we went back to the client SOP: it is the source of truth.
  • For each open point I brought two options and the trade-off, not one answer.
  • If the call went against me, I kept the other version ready, with the reason it might be needed later.

05 approach

Personas and flows first, UI last

I did not open Figma until every person and every hand-off was clear. AI made the first drafts fast. I made the decisions.

1. Understand

Turn the SOPs into rules, edge cases and open questions.

2. Define

A persona for each user, a journey for each team.

3. Flow

Task flows where every hand-off is a status.

4. Design

A small UI system, then the screens.

Where AI helped, and where it did not

  • Claude: read long SOPs and drafted personas, journeys, edge cases and copy in several tones.
  • Figma: flows, components and the final screens.
  • My job: check every line against the real process with the PO and CTO. AI drafts, I decide.

06 personas

Who I designed for

For the patient, I wrote one persona: a woman in her 50s with a serious, hard-to-treat cancer, a daily tablet for a year, no private insurance, and a daughter who helps with forms. Her lowest points were enrolling and waiting, so that is where the design work went.

07 the flow

One flow, two sides: a patient enrols, the Access team decides

If I could show only one thing, it would be this. The patient wants an answer fast. The Access team needs enough proof to say yes safely. I designed both sides as one flow, joined by status.

The patient side: four steps, a few minutes

Before: a long hospital-style form with documents patients could not find. After: phone, one ID, one tax form. Later orders reuse everything; she only uploads a receipt.

The Access side: review, decide, leave a trail

AFind new casesThe patient list opens on Pending, with counts per status, so nothing waits unseen.
BReview with everything in one placePrescription and means test side by side. Missing info goes to "Need more info", not a rejection.
CDecide, and the system does the restEligible creates the first order and the letter. Not eligible needs a reason. Both notify the patient.

08 the wall

Showing the client that two teams really stay apart

This is what won the pitch. I made the separation visible, in flows and in UI, so the client could see it instead of trusting a promise.

  • Separate journeys and task flows for each team. Data crosses only through a defined status, like "Received date".
  • Support calls show to Access only as who, when and how long. Never the content.
  • Support sees no files, no income, no scheme data.
  • One log grammar for every action, so any case can be audited on one page.

09 outcome

What happened

$50K+contract signed with the client
1designer for patient app, two portals and branding
MVPbuilt and handed to the client

The platform is new, so there are no usage numbers yet. These are the metrics I set up to track from launch:

  • Time to finish enrolment, and drop-off at each step.
  • Time from submit to an eligibility answer.
  • Follow-up completion (the client target is above 90%).
  • Patients still on treatment at month 6 (target above 70%).

10 what I learned

What I would take to any product

Words are design

In sensitive products, one wrong word breaks trust. Copy got as much care as layout.

Systems speed everyone up

One log grammar and one UI kit gave devs a spec and the CTO an audit view.

Evidence beats opinion

Next time I would test with 5 real users early, instead of only following the SOP.

How this maps to a marketplace
  • Minimum-field enrolment → shorter listing and sign-up forms
  • Eligibility review with a kind "no" → listing moderation with helpful rejections
  • Two separate portals → tools for sellers, buyers and CS
  • Audit logs → clear chat and deal history for disputes
  • Role-based masking → privacy and anti-scam, like masked phone numbers
  • Automated follow-ups → nudges to renew, reply and re-list
