02 context & problem

A vendor for pharma, and a patient who gives up

Big pharma companies run patient programs: they help people who cannot afford a treatment get it at a lower price, and support them while they take it. They hire vendors like us to build the platform and run the operations.

The business goal is simple to say: more eligible patients start the treatment and stay on it. The hard part is how. Everything must feel like support, never like selling, or the program breaks the law.

For the patient

She has a serious illness, no private insurance, and a doctor's prescription she cannot afford. To get help she faces hospital-style forms, documents she cannot find, and days of not knowing. Many give up before they get an answer.

For the client

Pharma must help patients without ever looking like it is selling. And it will not sign unless it can see that the team handling money and the team handling care never share data.

The problem in one line: how can a patient without insurance get into the program and get an answer as fast as possible, while two teams behind her stay provably apart?

So my goal: three users, ten documents, one wall

The patient

Very sick, often on a phone, paying out of pocket. Needs to enrol fast and know the outcome.

The Access team

Checks eligibility and money, creates orders. Must never see clinical calls.

The Support team

Nurses who follow up on doses and check-ups. Must never see income or documents.

The real question every pharma asks first: can you prove that Access data and Support data stay apart? Few platforms can. That proof decides the deal.

05 the flow

One flow, two sides: a patient enrols, the Access team decides

If I could show only one thing, it would be this. The patient wants an answer fast. The Access team needs enough proof to say yes safely. I designed both sides as one flow, joined by status.

The patient side: four steps, a few minutes

Before: a long hospital-style form with documents patients could not find. After: phone, one ID, one tax form. Later orders reuse everything; she only uploads a receipt.

06 handoff

Handoff to devs: one file, two lanes

Every flow ships from one Figma file. Handoff holds what is final; WIP holds change requests and new updates until they are agreed. A brief skill turns each feature into goals, constraints and edge cases before anyone builds.

07 handoff

How the design reached the developers

One Figma file, split by product. Each has a Handoff page with the final end-to-end flow, and a WIP page for change requests and new updates. Nothing moves to Handoff until it is agreed, so developers always know what is final.

For the brief that sits on top of each handoff, I use a design-handoff skill with Claude: user goal, constraints, edge cases and what is out of scope, written before the screens.

08 outcome & learning

What happened

$XXKcontract signed with the client
1designer for patient app, two portals and branding
MVPbuilt and handed to the client

The platform is new, so there are no usage numbers yet. These are the metrics I set up to track from launch:

  • Time to finish enrolment, and drop-off at each step.
  • Time from submit to an eligibility answer.
  • Follow-up completion (the client target is above 90%).
  • Patients still on treatment at month 6 (target above 70%).

What I learned

Words are design

In sensitive products, one wrong word breaks trust. Copy got as much care as layout.

Systems speed everyone up

One log grammar and one UI kit gave devs a spec and the CTO an audit view.

Evidence beats opinion

Next time I would test with 5 real users early, instead of only following the SOP.
